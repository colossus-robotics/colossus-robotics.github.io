Colossus

US-made robot joints from self-building factories.

  1. The rule. Since July 28, 2026, a new humanoid or other mobile robot can’t get the FCC authorization it needs to be sold in the US unless it’s built here with more than 65% US-made parts by cost, or gets a Conditional Approval from the Department of War.
  2. The part. Joints are about half the cost of a humanoid’s parts, and the gear inside each joint is the hardest part to get.
  3. The plan. Make US joints in small, identical factory cells that also build parts for the next cell, so capacity compounds.

The robot that can’t be sold here

On July 28, 2026, the FCC added foreign-produced humanoids, quadrupeds (robot dogs) and other mobile robots to its Covered List of equipment judged a national-security risk; fixed industrial arms aren’t on it. A new model now needs FCC authorization to be imported, marketed or sold in the US. It can get one as a domestic end product (built here, with US-made parts above 65% of its parts cost) or with a Conditional Approval. A robot assembled here from mostly foreign parts still counts as foreign. From 2029, the bar rises to 75%.

  1. The test counts component cost. Final-assembly labor doesn’t count.
  2. A component made in the US counts in full: its purchase price, or, if we make it, its cost to make (not counting profit).
  3. There is no allied-country carve-out. A Japanese or Korean part counts as foreign.
  4. Models authorized before July 28, 2026 can still be sold, but they’re frozen: no hardware changes.
  5. Conditional Approvals come from the Department of War (the Pentagon). Seven makers had one by late October 2026, each tied to a plan to move production to the US. Either path pushes robot makers toward US production.
  6. A Morgan Stanley estimate, cited by McKinsey, says building Tesla’s Optimus Gen 2 without Chinese suppliers would nearly triple its bill of materials (the cost of its parts).

NoteWith foreign joints the ceiling is 50%. No US joints, no US-made robot.

The joint

Our first product would be a strain-wave rotary joint for humanoid shoulders and elbows, like the one circled on the robot. It packs the gear, a frameless motor (a bare rotor and stator), encoders (angle sensors) and a driver (the control board) into one module, with an output bearing that carries the load.

We’d cut and heat-treat the gear in the US and buy the rest. The driver and bearing are vitamins, parts we’ll keep buying for good. The motor and encoders are bought for now.

NoteThe gear is 30–50% of the joint’s cost and the hardest part to source.

B1:1Detail B · Scale 1:1 · Top view

The gear

  1. The motor spins the oval cam fast.
  2. The cam bends the thin, toothed cup into an oval, so its teeth mesh with the fixed ring at two spots.
  3. The cup has 2 fewer teeth than the ring, so each spin of the cam moves the cup back by 2 teeth.
  4. Fast, weak spin goes in; slow, strong turning comes out. That is what a robot joint needs.

US robot makers are short of gears. Precision robot gears come from a few makers: Harmonic Drive and Nabtesco in Japan, Leaderdrive in China. They take great precision and costly equipment to make, and they are harder to scale than electronics (McKinsey).

Some are made here already: Cone Drive sells US-made strain-wave gearing from Michigan, and Harmonic Drive has a US plant in Massachusetts. Schaeffler plans formed (pressed, not cut) strain-wave gears at scale from 2027, starting in Germany. Our bet is capacity: identical cells that help build the next cell should add capacity faster than one conventional plant.

That shortage is why we’d make the gear here. On our reading of the federal definitions, a joint built in the US counts in full as a US-made component, even with an imported gear inside, so the rule alone doesn’t require a US gear. The FCC hasn’t ruled on this.

The cell that would make it

Pull back from the gear to the floor it would be made on. A production cell would be one machining center, one gear-cutting station, one heat-treat furnace, two robot arms that load the machines and assemble joints, and one inspection and test station.

The next cell

A cell’s arms would run on joints like ours, so a cell could make part of the next cell itself. Each joint would go one of two ways. Sold joints bring in cash, which buys the parts we can’t make yet. Kept joints go into the next cell’s arms. When the kit is full, it becomes a new cell.

Caveat. A joint we keep is one we don’t sell. Keeping it saves cash only when we have spare capacity, or when the part can’t be bought.

How much of the next cell our cells make

Closure is the share of a new cell’s value that our own cells make. Each rung of the ladder is one more thing the cells learn to make for the next cell. Tap a rung to bring it in-house, or climb them all. Vitamins stay bought.

Why not 100%? Vitamins such as chips and magnets come from huge, specialized plants. Buying them costs far less than building those plants, so they stay bought.

Four gates

The plan runs in phases. Each phase ends at a gate: a fact that has to become true before the next one starts.

First milestoneA strain-wave gear cut and heat-treated in the US, built into a joint that passes testing.

  1. Gate 1Three paying robot makers.Phase 1 · selling joints
  2. Gate 2One cell builds 40%+ of a new cell’s value.Phase 2 · self-built cells
  3. Gate 3Capacity doubles in 18 months or less on operating cash.Phase 3 · American arms
  4. Gate 4Cells make their own linear axes and some machine-tool modules.Phase 4 · a network of cells

After that: seed factories, small units that start production from local materials.
